## Activation · The Hinge — Q1: Two Failures, Not One
*8:06*

| Implementation failure | Failure to leverage |
| --- | --- |
| The system does not work. It crashes, loses data, cannot go live, is abandoned. Everyone sees it; everyone agrees it failed. Comparatively rare. | The system works — and value never arrives. ## Activation · The Terrain — Q2: Why ERP Is the Hardest Thing a Company Does
*8:08*

The largest, least-rehearsable project most firms ever run — and the software is rarely the root cause. The failure clusters in human and organizational choices.

- **Legacy, multi-system reality** — A strong core backbone that must integrate with specialized systems — not absorb them. The seams, not the systems, are where value leaks.
- **The delivery ecosystem** — Integrators, internal IT, the vendor, and the business stakeholders who own the process. Choosing a partner is high-leverage; lowest price costs more later.
- **Retained design authority** — Lean too hard on the vendor — or accept the platform's defaults — and you abdicate choices only the business can make. Stay the author of how your company works.
- **Integration on-ramps** — Forms, email, file transfer, XML, APIs. Natural on-ramps raise adoption; miss them and work routes around the backbone, re-creating silos.

**Transformation, not IT project.** The technology is the easy part; the organization is the hard part.

## Activation · The Cure — Q3: Don't Automate, Obliterate — But Selectively
*8:10*

Hammer, 1990: stop paving the cow paths — redesign the work from a blank sheet before you automate it. But "obliterate" is not "redesign everything." Concentrate total redesign where value is created or destroyed; adopt the standard where it isn't.

And decide the hard things early. Suppliers, ecosystem, requirements — the costliest to reverse, so make them when they are cheapest.

> [!info] Figure 1
> The cost of changing a decision rises steeply as scope and commitments grow. Adapted from Boehm's cost-of-change curve (1981).

The deeper target: replace batch-push planning (overnight plans, morning firefighting) with pull-based, event-driven operations — decisions made at the point of variance. That is the redesign that makes automation, and later agents, actually pay.

## Reconnect · The Promise — The Leverage Gap

| A backbone that works | A backbone that pays |
| --- | --- |
| Processes transactions. Stays up. Passes its tests. Technically, a success. | Turns activity into value the organization can actually keep. Far rarer — and the whole point. |

## Story · Primary Case — Birmingham: the Cutover That Erased the Books
*8:22*

> [!example]- Birmingham City Council — Oracle Cloud Fusion cutover
> | | |
> | --- | --- |
> | **April 2022** | Birmingham City Council — the largest local authority in Europe — went live on Oracle Cloud Fusion, replacing a long-standing SAP system, to run finance and HR. |
> | **The choice** | Rather than re-engineering finance and HR to fit a modern platform, the council bent the platform to its legacy ways of working. |
> | **What broke** | It posted transactions incorrectly and could not produce reliable accounts — the council lost its single source of truth. Costs escalated far beyond a modest business case. |
>
> **5 September 2023** — a Section 114 notice. The local-government equivalent of declaring it cannot balance its budget. Lose the single source of truth and you lose the ability to account for yourself.

## Story · The Dispute — Birmingham, and Here It Gets Hard
*8:24*

> [!example]- Birmingham — what actually caused the Section 114 (contested)
> What actually caused the Section 114 is genuinely contested — and the contest is the lesson.
>
> - **The council** — Attributed the notice to its long-running equal-pay liability and an in-year budget gap. Its statement did not name Oracle at all.
> - **Audit Reform Lab** — Reading the same accounts, argues nearly the reverse — that "Oracle seems the more likely explanation of Birmingham's problems."
> - **The external auditor** — A middle line — the Oracle failure as a contributory factor, faulting governance and optimism rather than the core technology.
> - **More recently** — Some experts argue the council may never have been truly bankrupt, on figures they call misstated; the commissioners reject that reading.
>
> Why can experts still disagree? Because the failed system left the council unable to produce reliable numbers about its own position. Lose the single source of truth and you lose the ability to account for yourself.

## Story · Supporting Case — Nike, 2000: the Same Trap, in the Clear
*8:25*

> [!example]- Nike i2 demand-planning rollout
> **What broke** — An i2 demand-planning engine bolted onto unredesigned processes, under-tested, rushed live in a Big Bang with under-trained planners. Forecast errors flowed straight into factory orders. Too many Air Garnetts; too few Air Jordans that were actually selling. Orders ignored, duplicated, and deleted — planners could not reconstruct what they had told factories to make.
>
> **What it cost — the damage landed in the financials**
> - **~$100M** in lost sales Nike publicly attributed to the episode
> - **~20%** one-day drop in the stock, on a profit warning
> - **~$400M** the broader supply-chain program it was measured against
>
> The recovery proves the rule: Nike retrained planners and moved short-range planning back into its SAP backbone, grounded in actual orders — a move its CIO said let it "simplify some of our integration requirements."

## Breakout 1 — Locate the Leak

**Work through it, in order:**

1. Sketch the process end to end — a few boxes, from the first trigger to "done," is enough.
2. Walk it with each lens in turn — Frustration, Time, Cost, Quality — and note at least one value leak under each.
3. Tag every leak with the thread it rings: Customer, Operations, or Cash.
4. Circle the overlap — the leak that shows up under two or three lenses. That is where one fix pays three times.

**Output:** A Four-Lenses friction list for one process. **Time:** 12 min + 2 debrief.

Debrief: two or three teams name their sharpest leak and the thread it rings.

## Debrief — What a Good Friction List Shows
*9:11*

1. **Every lens fired** — If a lens found nothing, you looked at the database, not the work — go back to the people in the process.
2. **Threads named** — Each leak rings a thread: Frustration → Customer, Time → Operations, Cost → Cash. Quality rings all three.
3. **The overlap found** — The leak that shows up under Quality and again under Time and Cost is where redesign pays three times — that is your candidate.

**Carry forward.** Your sharpest, thread-crossing leak is the one Breakout 2 will decide whether to obliterate.

## Breakout 2 — Obliterate, Selectively
*9:13–9:37*

From your friction list, decide where to redesign — and, just as important, where to leave things alone.

**Work through it, in order:**

1. Pick the one or two leaks where value is genuinely created or destroyed — and say why those, not everything.
2. For each, ask Hammer's prior question: should this step exist in this shape at all? Decide — total redesign, or incremental improvement.
3. Sketch the redesigned flow; name what you would obliterate vs. what you would adopt as the platform standard and leave alone.
4. Write one sentence: "Our backbone fails to leverage at ___, costing ___."

**Output:** A selective-redesign call: obliterate here, adopt-standard there. **Time:** 18 min + present.

Present: each team shows its one redesign call — this is a capstone piece, so it is presented, not just discussed.

## Debrief — Selective, Not Total
*9:31*

1. **Concentrated** — The strongest calls redesign one or two things and leave working areas alone — obliterating everything fails for the opposite reason to Nike and Birmingham.
2. **Standard where it doesn't drive outcome** — Adopt the platform's standard for the parts that don't create or destroy value; spend redesign only where it does.
3. **The rhythm, not just the steps** — The deepest redesigns replace batch-push habits with event-driven decisions at the point of variance.

**Retained design authority.** You stayed the author of how the company works — you did not let the platform's defaults decide for you.

## Breakout 3 — Make It Stick — and Make the Case
*9:37–9:58*

Turn the redesign into something an organization will adopt and a board will fund.

**Work through it, in order:**

1. Name who inside the process must help diagnose and reshape it — start from the Frustration lens. Adoption is designed in, not bolted on.
2. Name the standardization tax your redesign imposes — whose autonomy or roles it strips — and how you would soften it.
3. Decide how you would phase the rollout: progressive vs. Big Bang, and why.
4. Write the one-line leverage case — the sentence a board would fund. Not "install X" but "redesign Y so the business changes."

**Output:** An adoption + rollout note, and the one-line leverage case — not an installation case. **Time:** 15 min + present.

Present: each team reads its leverage case aloud — one sentence a board would fund.
