## Course Description

This is a semester-long course worth 3 credit hours, meeting Fridays 8:00–10:30 AM from September 4 through December 11, 2026 — 14 teaching sessions in total.

This course provides a comprehensive and strategic view of enterprise technology integration, designed to equip the next generation of leaders and technologists with a differentiating capability: the ability to design, integrate, and leverage complex business systems across strategic, operational, and human dimensions.

In today's economy, the Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) system is not a competitive advantage — it is a table stake. The true competitive advantage lies in what comes next: successful integration and optimisation, unlocking the strategic potential that remains untapped in most organisations' technology backbones. This course treats ERP as the non-negotiable foundation and builds upward through Robotic Process Automation (RPA), Intelligent Automation, and ultimately autonomous AI agents.

The foundational challenge is universal regardless of company size: how do you create a reliable, integrated data backbone from which to build progressive layers of automation? A Fortune 500 company with a $50 million SAP implementation and a 30-person startup stitching together Salesforce, QuickBooks, and Shopify face identical strategic questions. Where this syllabus refers to 'ERP,' students working in smaller organisations should read 'integration backbone.'

To make this concrete, the course works with four recurring company archetypes: a Consumer Packaged Goods (CPG) manufacturer running S&OP off SAP; a mid-market manufacturer using NetSuite for Order-to-Cash; a Series B startup running order-to-cash across Stripe, Shopify, and QuickBooks; and a 50-person services firm operating on HubSpot and Notion. These four archetypes travel through the full course and give you a portable mental model across every scale of business — from Fortune 500 to seed-stage. Wherever this syllabus refers to 'ERP,' locate your company on this scale and read accordingly.

The business problems this course is designed to address fall into three interrelated threads that run through every module and every lab:

- **Customer** — how integrated systems enable order fulfilment, service quality, and customer experience at scale.
- **Operations & Supply Chain** — how production planning, inventory management, and process synchronisation determine whether a company can deliver on its promises.
- **Cash Management** — how the speed and reliability of the integration backbone determines how fast cash moves through the business.

Cash flow is a leading reason businesses fail. Value growth is a leading reason they succeed. This course teaches you how integration and automation directly move both levers — and how agentic AI is creating the next wave of competitive separation for companies that get this right.

This is not only a technology course. It teaches a replicable methodology — problem framing, process diagnosis, solution design, governance, and board-ready communication — that you can deploy professionally from the moment you complete it. The capstone project is structured as a strategic proof of concept: your team picks a real company, identifies the integration and automation opportunity most likely to move the needle on one of the three threads, and builds a board-ready investment case across four progressive labs.

**Learn-by-Building:** A distinctive feature of this course is its learn-by-building philosophy. Students complete one structured lab per module, using accessible no-code platforms to build and break a process map, an automated workflow, an AI agent, and a governance brief. These four artifacts are progressive — each one builds on the previous — and together they form the evidence base for the final deliverables.

The curriculum is structured around three core pillars present in every module:

- **Foundational Process Integration:** How a reliable data backbone provides the single source of truth and defines the fundamental constraints and opportunities for automation.
- **Strategic Business Integration:** How to layer automation intelligently — from RPA to Agentic AI — on that foundation to create measurable business value.
- **Change Leadership & Ethics:** The managerial and ethical frameworks required to lead people through radical technological change, addressed in every module.

## Course Objectives

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

- **Apply a Replicable Methodology:** Use the course framework to diagnose integration and automation opportunities across Customer, Operations & Supply Chain, and Cash Management — and produce board-ready investment cases that quantify value, cost, and risk.
- **Evaluate Strategic Leverage:** Articulate how a well-integrated technology backbone serves as the foundation for the modern enterprise and how to leverage it for continuous strategic enablement.
- **Design Transformation Blueprints:** Design a multi-layered digital transformation roadmap identifying appropriate technologies — from RPA to Agentic AI — layered on the integration foundation.
- **Model and Redesign Processes:** Analyse and redesign core business processes (BPR) optimised for system integration and both rules-based and cognitive automation.
- **Build and Evaluate Agents:** Deploy, test, and critically evaluate AI agents using accessible no-code platforms, identifying failure modes and governance requirements from direct experience.
- **Govern Autonomous Systems:** Examine the ethical, risk, and governance frameworks necessary to safely deploy and manage multi-agent AI systems in enterprise and startup contexts.
- **Lead Organisational Change:** Develop a methodology for diagnosing, leading, and sustaining technology-driven change, recognising that integration success is primarily a function of human and organisational alignment.

### MODULE 1: The Integration Foundation | Weeks 1–3 | Sep 4–18

This module establishes the non-negotiable foundation of the modern enterprise: a reliable, integrated technology backbone. We examine how ERPs and SaaS stacks enforce process standardisation, where they fail to deliver on their promise, and how Business Process Re-engineering (BPR) prepares an organisation for automation. The deeper target of BPR is not just eliminating process steps — it is replacing the underlying planning paradigm: most ERPs are still run as batch-push systems where Materials Requirements Planning (MRP) runs overnight, plans cascade downstream, and humans firefight exceptions in the morning. The precondition for automation to deliver real value is shifting toward near-real-time, event-driven operations where decisions happen at the point of variance. The three core threads — Customer, Operations & Supply Chain, and Cash Management — all run through the integration backbone: customer orders flow through Order to Cash (O2C), supply plans run through MRP and Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP), and cash conversion depends on how fast and accurately the backbone processes transactions. Module 1 teaches you to read a company's integration stack as a diagnostic of all three.

#### Week 0 (Pre-Course Primer): The ERP Floor — before Friday, September 4

- **In brief** — A pre-course primer that sets the ERP floor and the course's tone before Week 1. It covers ERP basics and how ERP types differ, a BPMN / process-mapping primer. This is a study-note primer (notes teach), not mandated textbook reading.
- **Concepts taught** — What an ERP / integration backbone is; the range of ERP types and how their characteristics differ; the shared data model (finance, procurement, inventory, manufacturing, HR); a BPMN / process-mapping primer.
- **Reading set** — Tone-setter: McKinsey, 'Bridging the great AI agent and ERP divide to unlock value at scale' (Jan 2026, open access). Foundation (classic): Davenport, 'Putting the Enterprise into the Enterprise System' (HBR, July–Aug 1998; NYU Libraries) — the classic that defined the problem, not current-state. Supporting video: 'An explanation of SAP ERP, ECC and S/4HANA' (Eye on Tech / Informa TechTarget). Optional: SAP, 'What is ERP: The Essential Guide.'

#### Week 1: Introduction: The Integration Backbone — Friday, September 4

- **In brief** — The integration backbone — ERP or a multi-SaaS stack — is the single source of truth and the non-negotiable table stake for modern operations at any scale. With the ERP floor set in Week 0, this week defines what the backbone is and why it so often fails to deliver value even when it works technically.
- **Objectives** — Define the backbone as single source of truth and process enforcer; read O2C and P2P as the flows every integration touches; locate control points and decision points; frame backbone work as a business transformation, not an IT project.
- **Concepts taught** — The integration backbone (single source of truth); Order-to-Cash and Procure-to-Pay; control points and decision points; the leverage gap; packaged ERP versus custom-built backbones; the Four Lenses (previewed). (ERP anatomy is covered in the Week 0 primer; the delivery ecosystem and legacy reality are covered in Week 2.)
- **Assigned case** — Primary case: NYU Bobst Library / Jaggaer migration (2022), grounded in Ferguson (2025), ALA LRTS — peer-reviewed, open access. Supporting: Lidl / SAP "eLWIS" (~€500M, abandoned 2018 after ~7 years), from Computer Weekly and Handelsblatt; the enterprise-scale counterpart to Bobst's small-scale integration gap, showing standardization-vs-local-fit failure.
- **Deliverable → Board Memo** — §2 (Context & Background): confirm your company by end of Week 1 and draft a first backbone-context note (stack, key processes, archetype, primary thread). You also receive the capstone storyboard this week and map your team's final board deck to it, so each week's homework fills a defined piece of the final presentation.
- **Teams & setup** — You receive your team assignment after this first class; engage with your teammates once teams are formed, and set up free accounts for the default-stack tools (Miro/Lucidchart, Airtable/Google Sheets, Gemini) during the first week.
- **Go deeper (optional)** — Ross & Weill Ch. 1–2 (foundation of execution + operating models); Westerman, Introduction. Optional: Madison on process mapping.

#### Week 2: Failure to Leverage + BPR as Remedy — Friday, September 11

- **In brief** — A technically 'successful' ERP can still fail to deliver value; the remedy is process redesign before automation, not better technology. Nike shows the failure mode; Hammer's 'obliterate, then automate' and the Four Lenses show the fix.
- **Objectives** — Distinguish leverage failure from implementation failure; sequence BPR ahead of automation; locate value leaks with the Four Lenses; decide where total redesign is warranted and where incremental change suffices; embed adoption from the start of a redesign.
- **Concepts taught** — Leverage failure; Business Process Re-engineering (Hammer); selective redesign — total versus incremental change, concentrating on the few areas where value is created or destroyed rather than reshaping the business to fit the platform; the ERP delivery ecosystem and integration on-ramps; vendor-involvement risk and retained design authority; the legacy / multi-system reality; batch-push vs. event-driven operations; the Four Lenses (Frustration, Time, Cost, Quality); adoption over change management; Lab 1 pre-work (spans W2–3).
- **Assigned case** — Primary case: Birmingham City Council / Oracle (2022–23) — s114 notice + Grant Thornton audit + Sheffield Audit Reform Lab. Supporting: Nike i2 supply-chain failure (2000) — Koch, "Nike Rebounds" (CIO, 2004); the Dolfing blog is a further supporting reference.
- **Deliverable → Board Memo** — §2 (Context & Background): a one-page context-and-diagnosis brief on your company's current backbone and where it fails to leverage. Also due end of Week 2: your team charter (template on Brightspace).
- **Go deeper (optional)** — Ross & Weill develop the operating-model choice in Ch. 2–3; Hammer's 1990 HBR essay is the origin of 'obliterate, then automate.'

#### ★ Week 3: LAB 1 — Diagnose & Map a Process — Friday, September 18

- **In brief** — A two-part lab spanning Weeks 2–3. You research a company's real pain (gated pre-work brief), then in class map its process and control points, absorb a curveball, and generate synthetic data last — business first, data last.
- **Objectives** — Diagnose friction with the Four Lenses; map an end-to-end process, its control points and its decision points; identify the best RPA candidate step and the step that needs AI judgment.
- **Concepts applied** — Friction-chain diagnosis; control points and decision points; the Four Lenses; RPA-candidate vs. AI-judgment steps; rejection and rework paths; the synthetic-data sandbox.
- **Reading** — set up the lab platform before class (accounts are free); the pre-work research brief is due and checked before class.
- **Deliverable → Board Memo** — §3 (Backbone Assessment) — Milestone 1 (problem diagnosis): research brief + annotated process map (control points, three diagnostic points, friction chain) + Gemini-built sandbox.
- **Go deeper (optional)** — set up the lab platform before class.

### MODULE 2: The Automation Ladder | Weeks 4–6 | Sep 25–Oct 9

This module builds the automation capability layer: from first-generation RPA through scaled enterprise deployment, governed by a Center of Excellence. Lab 2 produces a working automation scenario that connects at least two systems. The primary thread in Module 2 is Operations & Supply Chain — RPA finds its first traction in repetitive, rules-based operational processes — but every automation decision has a Cash impact (cycle time = cash conversion speed) and a Customer impact (order accuracy = customer experience).

#### Week 4: RPA: The Tactical Integration Layer — Friday, September 25

- **In brief** — RPA is the first, tactical layer of automation on the backbone. This week defines it, separates it from AI, and shows where it first finds traction — and where its limits appear.
- **Objectives** — Define RPA and distinguish it from traditional automation and AI; identify high-value RPA candidates; avoid automating a broken process; read a working O2C baseline.
- **Concepts taught** — RPA vs. traditional automation vs. AI; the RPA business case and candidate selection; MRP/S&OP batch-push planning; the working-backbone baseline.
- **Assigned case** — Primary case: Thermo Fisher Scientific RPA (CIO.com). Supporting: Bailey Hydraulics / NetSuite O2C, a working-backbone baseline.
- **Deliverable → Board Memo** — §4 (Automation Journey): a short RPA-candidate scan for your company — which repetitive, rules-based steps qualify, and why.
- **Go deeper (optional)** — Tella et al. Ch. 1–2 (Automation + Process Mindset); Deloitte, *Automation with intelligence* (2022); MRP/S&OP context: Orlicky (1975), Ling & Goddard (1988).

#### Week 5: Scaling RPA + Center of Excellence — Friday, October 2

- **In brief** — Most RPA programmes stall at a handful of bots. A Center of Excellence governs the jump from pilot to enterprise scale — and defines the human roles that keep automation sustainable.
- **Objectives** — Explain why RPA programmes stall at scale; design a CoE operating model and governance; place the human roles in a scaled programme.
- **Concepts taught** — Scaling limits of RPA; the Center of Excellence (operating model, governance, bot ownership); selector instability and integration drift; building automation alongside the workforce.
- **Assigned case** — Primary case: Telefónica O2 RPA (Lacity & Willcocks, MIS Quarterly Executive, 2016; open-access working paper) — a peer-reviewed account of scaling RPA through a small in-house team and a Back-Office-owned operating model. Supporting: Orange Spain 'Robot Factory' (UiPath case study; vendor-reported), a second telecom Center of Excellence for illustration.
- **Deliverable → Board Memo** — §4 (Automation Journey): a draft CoE / operating-model section for your company.
- **Go deeper (optional)** — Tella et al. Ch. 4–5 (Scale Mindset + Orchestration); Westerman Ch. 3 (Core Operations).

#### ★ Week 6: LAB 2 — Automate the Repeatable Part — Friday, October 9

- **In brief** — Build a working multi-step automation that connects at least two systems for your company — then break it. Finding where it breaks is the governance lesson.
- **Objectives** — Build a multi-step automation in Make; connect two cloud services; document a failure mode and the human oversight it implies.
- **Concepts applied** — Trigger–condition–action workflows; multi-system hand-offs; failure modes and oversight; simple ROI of automation.
- **Reading** — Make.
- **Deliverable → Board Memo** — §4 (Automation Journey) — completes Milestone 1: a working automation + a one-page post-mortem (what broke, oversight, ROI).
- **Go deeper (optional)** — set up Make before class.

### MODULE 3: Intelligent Automation + AI Agents | Weeks 7–10 | Oct 16–Nov 6

This module moves from rule-based automation to AI-native systems. We examine the architectural shift from RPA to intelligent automation, the SPAR framework for AI agent design, and the governance challenges of deploying systems that can act autonomously. Lab 3 is the centrepiece: building and deliberately breaking an AI agent. Module 3 is where the Customer thread becomes primary: AI agents are being deployed first at customer-facing interfaces (service, fulfilment, dispute resolution) because that is where the ROI is most visible and the governance stakes are highest.

#### Week 7: From RPA to Intelligent Automation — Friday, October 16

- **In brief** — Where rule-based automation hits its ceiling and cognitive technologies (NLP, ML) take over — the shift from automating tasks to automating decisions, and what it demands of governance.
- **Objectives** — Identify what rules-based RPA cannot do; explain the shift to event-driven, intelligent automation; articulate the governance change when AI replaces expert judgment.
- **Concepts taught** — Limits of rules-based systems; NLP/ML for unstructured inputs; event-driven vs. batch-push architectures; the Enterprise AI Platform; process intelligence.
- **Assigned case** — Primary case: JPMorgan COiN (Contract Intelligence) — Son, 'JPMorgan Software Does in Seconds What Took Lawyers 360,000 Hours' (Bloomberg, 2017), journalism of record; open-access report via ABA Journal (2017).
- **Deliverable → Board Memo** — §5 (Agent Design & Governance): identify the one step in your process that needs AI judgment (your Lab 3 target) and why rules fail there.
- **Go deeper (optional)** — Tella et al. Ch. 15 (Enterprise AI Platform); Iansiti & Lakhani Ch. 3 (AI Factory); Bornet et al. Ch. 1–2 (pre-read for W8).

## Course Policies

### Generative Artificial Intelligence Use

This course has a deliberately two-sided relationship with AI: you will use it, and you will build with it. The distinction that governs academic integrity here is not which tools you touch, but who authors the design.

**AI as a thinking partner (permitted, with disclosure).** You may use AI tools — Gemini or any comparable platform — for research assistance, brainstorming, and drafting support. When you do, disclose it in a brief statement at the end of the submission. All analysis, arguments, and conclusions you submit for grading must represent your own thinking.

**AI as a builder (your design must be your own).** Your lab artifacts must reflect your own design decisions and hands-on platform work. Because each team chooses its own stack — the Default no-code path, the partner-supported AWS path, or a student-designed custom stack — the boundary is defined by authorship of the design, not by a list of tools. Submitting AI-generated workflow configurations or agent architectures — on any platform, whether Make, Relevance AI, AWS Step Functions, Amazon Bedrock, or a tool of your own choosing — without substantial personal design and modification constitutes academic misconduct.

The course itself builds AI agents, so the line is not about avoiding AI; it is about owning the design. Use AI to think and to build — but the architecture, the decisions, and the judgment must be yours, and you must be able to explain and defend them.

### Late Work

Lab artifacts are due EOD of the lab week. Because each lab feeds the next, late submission has downstream consequences for your team. Late lab artifacts are accepted up to one week after the deadline with a 10-point deduction. The agent design brief and governance draft (due before Week 10) are hard deadlines — the checkpoint cannot proceed without them. The final presentation (December 11) and board memo (December 14) are absolute deadlines; no late submissions accepted.
