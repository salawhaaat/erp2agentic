Your Capstone Kit

How the pieces fit — and how to use them

Across this course your team carries one real business process from diagnosis to a board-
ready transformation argument — built in stages (backbone diagnosis, automation, AI agent,
three-phase roadmap) and presented twice at the end: an Executive Board Memo and a board
presentation. This kit is the set of tools you use to build it: three pieces, three jobs.

1  The Capstone Storyboard — your map.  It lays out all fourteen sessions and shows what
each week's homework and lab feeds in the final deck and memo. Read it in Week 1 to see the
end-state from the start — every piece of work has a place it belongs.

2  The Board Memo & Board Presentation templates — your containers.  You fill these as
the term goes, not at the end. Every section names the lesson, lab, or homework that produces
its content, so the memo and deck accumulate week by week. The two are one argument told
twice — each deck slide is tagged with the memo section it backs. The presentation template
also flags where a diagram or chart beats words; how you show it is your call.

3  The worked example — your mirror.  One company — Citi Bike's rebalancing operation —
runs through the whole capstone as a model of strong work at each stage. Each piece arrives
after you have done that stage yourself, so you use it to sharpen your own thinking, not to copy
— the point is the shape, not the specifics. Citi Bike is off-limits as a team choice.

How they connect

The course climbs one idea to a higher altitude each module — integrate, automate, make
intelligent, make agentic — along three threads: Customer, Operations & Supply Chain, and
Cash Management. The small, sharp problem you diagnose in Week 3 is the one you will still
argue, at board level, in December. Lab 1 becomes your diagnosis, Lab 2 your automation, Lab
3 your agent, Lab 4 your roadmap — together filling the seven memo sections and thirteen deck
slides.

How to work with the kit

●  Fill the templates as each week lands — don't leave the capstone to December.
●  Write the memo's Executive Summary last.
●  The deck says what to cover, not how — favour a diagram, a chart, or one clear number over

bullets.

●  Treat the worked example as a mirror: read each stage after your own attempt, to sharpen it,

not to copy.

●  Deck and memo are one argument — keep them consistent as they grow.
