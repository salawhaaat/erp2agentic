## Integrate the Backbone First
*(8:21)*
**Every company runs on a nervous system**

- An ERP is the shared data backbone connecting every function — finance, procurement, inventory, production, customer orders (Finance, Procurement, Inventory, Orders all tied into one ERP core).
- When it works, a single sale automatically triggers procurement, adjusts stock, and moves cash. No re-keying.
- When it doesn't, people re-enter data between disconnected systems — orders go missing, cash stalls, and automation amplifies the dysfunction instead of fixing it.

Most companies' nervous systems are broken. Systems integration is the discipline of diagnosing what's broken and sequencing the fix — before any automation is layered on top.

## Same Idea, Several Shapes
*(8:23)*
**"ERP" is not one thing**

A backbone holds four broad zones — leave one out and the company has a blind spot exactly there:

- Financial
- Human-capital
- Operations & logistics
- Commercial

And backbones differ along three market dimensions — cutting across the four archetypes' size scale:

| Dimension | Range |
| --- | --- |
| Deployment | On-premise, cloud, or hybrid. |
| Scope | Generalist / horizontal vs industry-specific / vertical. |
| Market tier | Tier I (large global), II (mid-market), III (small business). |

Reading a backbone means placing it on these lines — not only on the size scale. Is your company cloud or on-prem, generalist or vertical, which tier? Job ads and vendor pages give most of it away.

## One Fact, One Place — and the Process That Guards It
*(8:26)*

- Strip the backbone to its defining property: a single source of truth. For any fact — an order quantity, an invoice status, a stock level — exactly one authoritative record everyone reads from and writes to.
- The shared data model makes it possible: "customer 4471" means the same entity to finance and to fulfillment.
- **When it's absent** — every department quietly keeps its own version of reality, and the endless work of making the copies agree becomes the hidden full-time job.
- A real backbone also enforces the process: it won't post an invoice against an unapproved order, or ship goods never reserved.

Single source of truth and process enforcer are inseparable. The truth is trustworthy only if the steps that created it were followed in order. That's why the backbone is both at once.

## Two Flows Run Through Every Integration Decision
*(8:29)*

**O2C — Order-to-Cash** — a customer's order, all the way to cash in the door.

| Order | Fulfil / ship | Invoice | Cash collected |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

**P2P — Procure-to-Pay** — a requisition, all the way to a supplier paid. (Bobst runs through this flow today.)

| Requisition | Purchase order | Receipt | Supplier paid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

Learn their names now and watch for them all term. Almost every integration decision touches one of these two end-to-end flows. When you diagnose a company, you trace the flow first.

## Where Data Is Born
*(8:32)*
**Control points: where the business commits**

- Trace either flow and a few moments matter far more than the rest: a PO is issued, a serial or lot number generated, credit approved, an invoice posted.
- These are control points — the instants where the business commits and a new, authoritative fact comes into existence.
- They are where governance attaches, where an error is cheapest to catch, and — later in the course — where automation and AI agents plug in.
- So we read the process before the data. Data hangs on a process, not the other way round. Business first, data last.

A control point is where data is born. Find them first: in Lab 1 you'll mark them on your own process map. Map the business process and its control points first; fit the data to it last.

## Where the Path Forks
*(8:36)*
**Decision points — and the rework loop where value leaks**

| Order received | Credit check | Reserve & pick | Ship & serialize | Invoice posted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| control point (data born) | decision point (path forks) | | | |

Rejection loop: **REJECTED → manual rework → re-enter**

> BPMN basics: a task is a box, an arrow is sequence, a diamond gateway is a decision. That's all you need for Lab 1.

Rejection is the costly case. A rejected item doesn't stop — it's pushed back, fixed by hand, and re-enters only after rework. Each loop adds delay; each manual touch adds error. These loops are where value leaks fastest — and the richest targets in Lab 1. They're also where the course's later question gets decided: which steps a rule-based robot can handle, and which need genuine human judgment.

## The Organising Choice, Before Any Technology
*(8:43)*
**Two questions decide everything**

Before it buys a thing, a company answers two separate questions about how it means to operate — and too often blurs them into one:

**1 · Standardization** — How identically should a process run across the company, whoever performs it and wherever? It drives out variability and lets you measure and improve. The price: it limits local innovation, and the switch is expensive and bruising.

**2 · Integration** — How much should units share data — so a transaction flows end to end, and the company shows one face to the customer? Its hardest part is the data: units must agree on common definitions before anything can flow.

Even the word "sale" isn't safe. One unit dates a sale from the signed contract, another from cash received, a third from delivery. Until they agree, there is no single source of truth to share.

> [!example]- Four Stances — and Four Real Companies That Took Them (8:48)
> Ross & Weill's operating-model matrix, plotted on two axes: **Standardization** (low → high, x-axis) and **Integration** (low → high, y-axis).
>
> | | Low standardization | High standardization |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | **High integration** | **Coordination** — Merrill Lynch, Global Private Client | **Unification** — Dow Chemical, one ERP, ~60% shared |
> | **Low integration** | **Diversification** — JM Family Enterprises, separate businesses | **Replication** — McDonald's, one formula, stamped everywhere |
>
> There is no best quadrant — only a stance that fits your strategy or fights it. Once chosen, the stance stops describing the company and starts driving it. Placing your company here is Board Memo Section 3.

## Why the Floor Matters
*(8:53)*
**The foundation that frees you**

- The paradox: digitizing a core process makes that one process less flexible — it's in software now — and yet it makes the whole company more agile.
- Think of an athlete: fixed muscles and trained reflexes are exactly what let them react and improvise. A firm that has digitized what doesn't change is free to focus on what does.
- The foundation for execution becomes a platform for innovation precisely by making the routine boring.

**~1/3** of firms had digitized their core — and enjoyed higher profitability, faster time to market, lower risk, and about a quarter lower IT costs.

The foundation is not a luxury that costs more — done right, it costs less. That's the case for treating the backbone as the main event, not an afterthought — the exact mistake this course exists to head off.

## How to Spot a Company That Lacks a Foundation
*(8:57)*

- Different parts of the company give different answers to the same customer question.
- Meeting a new regulation is a massive, top-driven scramble.
- Every new initiative feels like starting from scratch.
- IT is the perpetual bottleneck — and people spend their days moving data from one system into another.

Learn to hear these in the wild — in a case, an interview, your own employer. Each is evidence the foundation is weak, whatever the software cost. Westerman's study of 400+ large firms found the winners aren't the biggest spenders — the "Digital Masters" run ~26% more profitable, by pairing digital capability with the leadership to drive change.

## The Question the Whole Course Turns On
*(9:00)*
**A backbone can work — and still fail to pay**

- A company can select a backbone, implement it, survive go-live, and end up with a system that genuinely works — data flows, transactions post — and still find the business no better off.
- The promised savings don't materialise; the customer experience is unchanged; cash still moves at the old speed.
- The distance between a backbone that is deployed and one that delivers value is the **leverage gap**. Closing it is the real work of the next thirteen weeks.

**>70%** of recently implemented ERP initiatives will, by 2027, fail to fully meet their original business goals — up to a quarter of them catastrophically. *(Gartner)*

Read the number carefully. It isn't saying the software doesn't run. It's saying that most of the time, even a functioning system doesn't deliver what the business set out to get. That is the leverage gap, measured.

## A First Look at Friction: The Four Lenses
*(9:05)*

| Lens | What it looks for |
| --- | --- |
| **Frustration** | Where the work irritates the people doing it or the customer receiving it — a tell for hidden rework and workarounds. |
| **Time** | Where the process waits, queues, or doubles back — elapsed time far exceeding actual work time. |
| **Cost** | Where effort, rework, or error-correction consumes money out of proportion to the value the step adds. |
| **Quality** | Where errors and defects are produced — then caught and fixed somewhere downstream. |

A lens looks at a process, not a database. You find the leak in the work, then ask what data would prove it. We preview them today; the full diagnostic is Week 2, and you first apply them in the Week 2 pre-work and Lab 1.

> [!example]- A Backbone in Our Own Backyard — NYU Bobst Library: the Problem (9:08)
> *Primary case · Ferguson (2025), open access*
>
> - 2014–2021: Bobst paid most collections invoices through a homegrown workflow that bypassed iBuy, the university's standard procurement platform.
> - Staff ordered and received in the library's own system; a weekly job pushed invoice data straight to Accounts Payable.
> - To the people using it, it felt efficient.
>
> To everyone outside the library, it was opaque:
> - no record of who authorised the spending
> - no metadata on what was purchased
> - no copies of the invoices retained centrally
>
> A small case by design — the point is clarity, not scale: a Procure-to-Pay flow you can hold in your hand.

> [!example]- Bobst: Solving One Gap Opened Another (9:11)
> An internal audit finds that opacity and recommends adopting the standard platform, iBuy.
>
> **Early 2021** — audit and recommendation.
> **1 Jan 2022** — Bobst goes live on iBuy (Jaggaer), integrated with Oracle ERP (FAME). The opacity dissolves — every order carries its approver, invoices live centrally.
>
> **The twist:** iBuy and the library's own system aren't integrated with each other — so staff now enter each purchase twice.
>
> **~10 extra work-weeks in a single year** — purely from double entry.
>
> This is a missing foundation's textbook symptom. People moving data from one system into another — re-created at a new seam by a technically successful migration. Integration is a gap you keep closing, not a finish line.

> [!example]- Bobst: the Lesson, and the Thread It Lands On (9:13)
> - The backbone did its job right the first time: adopting the standard platform restored a single source of truth and pulled a rogue process back into the central flow.
> - And yet it re-created the very symptom of a missing foundation — daily data re-entry — at a new seam.
> - Integration is not a state you arrive at; it's a gap you keep closing, and closing one can open the next.
>
> **The thread this lands on:** Cash Management (primary); Operations (overlay) — the currency in which the double-entry cost is actually paid.
>
> This is exactly the diagnosis your team runs in the breakout. Trace the flow, mark where data is born and where it's re-keyed, and name what the seam costs — on a named thread. Bobst is your worked example.

> [!example]- The Same Lesson at Nine Figures — Lidl and SAP: Half a Billion Euros, No Value (9:16)
> *Supporting case · Computer Weekly + Handelsblatt*
>
> **€500M** reported spend · **2011 → 2018** (7 years) · **0** value delivered — reverted to legacy.
>
> - Not a story about bad software. Lidl values inventory at the price it paid; standard SAP for Retail is built around the retail price.
> - Faced with the mismatch, Lidl bent the software to its processes rather than its processes to the software — and the customization compounded until the program collapsed.
>
> Customize vs configure — the single most repeated ERP error. A configured system stays close to the vendor standard and survives upgrades; a heavily customized one drifts until routine updates break it. The backbone was deliverable but never adoptable.

> [!example]- Worked Example — Chipotle: Flow, Model, and the Leverage Gap (9:35)
> **4 · Trace one flow**
> A digital order (app / web / delivery) is routed to a restaurant, prepared on the make-line, and handed off in-store, at a Chipotlane, or to a courier — then payment is captured.
>
> - ★ **Control point:** order committed — payment authorized, the order record is born.
> - ◆ **Decision point:** at peak, accept the order now or delay it — capacity vs demand.

> **One-sentence output:** "Chipotle's backbone runs a standardized, company-owned operation well, but its fast-growing digital demand may outrun real-time kitchen and inventory sync — a throughput leak on the Operations and Customer threads." A gap stated as a hypothesis, ready to validate.

## Debrief: What a Good Read Sounds Like
*(10:07)*

- The single source of truth looks different at every scale — one SAP instance, three stitched SaaS tools, a shared spreadsheet held together by a person — but the failure mode is identical: without one authoritative record, reconciliation becomes a tax.
- There's no best quadrant. A stance that fits one company's strategy would fight another's.
